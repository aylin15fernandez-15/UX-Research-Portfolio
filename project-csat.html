Building a CSAT baseline across 9 internal products

The Regulatory Controls & Operational Risk Technology UX team lacked a standardized way to measure how users felt about the tools they relied on every day. I was brought in as an intern to design and help launch a CSAT program from scratch, establishing a repeatable measurement framework across the team's full product suite.

Scale
300+ participants · 9 products
Methods
Survey design · 5-point Likert scale · Qualitative analysis
Timeline
July 2025 – ongoing
My role
Researcher · Program design · Stakeholder readouts
Background

The problem

The UX team supporting Regulatory Controls & Operational Risk Technology at JPMorganChase had never systematically measured how users experienced the internal tools they supported. Without a baseline, there was no way to understand where products stood, track improvement over time, or make the case for research-informed changes.

My manager identified this gap and tasked me, at the start of my internship, with executing a standardized CSAT program that could be deployed consistently across all nine products in the team's portfolio.

Approach

Designing the framework

Each survey was designed to be short (under five minutes) so as not to burden users in already demanding operational roles. The core structure captured three quantitative variables on a 5-point Likert scale:

  • Satisfaction, a consistent anchor across all products
  • Ease of use, a consistent second variable across all products
  • A product-specific third variable, tailored per product based on what we knew about how users engaged with it

After the quantitative questions, each survey included an open-ended qualitative question. The qualitative responses gave us the context to understand what was driving the scores we saw and to explain them credibly to product stakeholders.

Surveys were distributed to users across each product's known user base, capturing response rates and completion rates alongside the scores themselves.

Findings

What the data showed

Baseline scores were collected across all nine products. Using SurveyMonkey's standard scoring benchmarks (0–50% needs improvement, 50–70% fair, 70–85% good, 85–100% excellent), the results were sobering: not a single product reached the "good" range. Most sat in the "fair" or "needs improvement" categories.

The data was real, and it was uncomfortable. But the more important task was making sure product owners didn't read it as a verdict. It was a starting point, and every score could only go up from here, so now we had the data to prove when it did.

The qualitative responses added critical depth. They surfaced specific friction points, like confusing workflows, missing features, and unclear labeling, that explained why scores landed where they did and gave product teams a clear direction for where to focus.

Delivery

Readouts and stakeholder management

For each product, I produced a one-page infographic readout that included project background, a by-the-numbers summary, the three quantitative scores with clear visual presentation, and our interpretation of the scores supported by qualitative themes.

These readouts were presented directly to product stakeholders, often product owners who hadn't previously engaged with formal user research. Managing that reaction was as much a part of the work as designing the surveys. The framing I consistently returned to: these scores give us a starting point, and they give us the evidence we need to demonstrate improvement over time.

For most products, this reframe worked. Stakeholders became more engaged, more interested in discussing roadmap timing, feature prioritization, and when it made sense to run a follow-up CSAT to measure change.

Impact

What this made possible

9

Products with documented baseline satisfaction scores for the first time

300+

Participants engaged across the program

Ongoing

Framework still in use, enabling longitudinal tracking of product improvement

Beyond the numbers, the program established a culture of measurement within a team that had none. Product teams now have a shared vocabulary for talking about user satisfaction, along with the infrastructure to demonstrate, with data, that research-informed changes are making a difference.
