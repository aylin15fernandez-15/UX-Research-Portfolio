Discovery Research · JPMorgan Chase

11 interviews that redirected an entire platform strategy

Before a single line of code was written, discovery research revealed that the product direction senior leaders had in mind would have created more problems than it solved. The research reoriented the entire strategy toward a solution that actually fit how teams worked.

Scale
11 interviews
Methods
Discovery interviews · Workflow mapping · Stakeholder readout
Timeline
2025
My role
Sole researcher · Interview design · Analysis · Readout delivery
Background

The problem

Reporting within Regulatory Controls & Operational Risk Technology was broken, and everyone knew it. There had been a previous attempt at a centralized reporting platform that had failed badly enough that it was abandoned entirely. Senior leaders were now weighing whether to try again: rebuild the tool, or find another path forward.

Before committing to any direction, they asked our team to understand how reporting actually worked on the ground, across the various sub-teams, before deciding what to build.

Approach

Getting into the work

I conducted 11 discovery interviews with a range of people involved in the reporting process: end users who needed reports, product stakeholders who managed requests, and the technical individuals who actually pulled and formatted the data. The goal was to map the full workflow as it actually existed, not as it was designed to work.

Interview questions focused on how reporting requests were initiated, how they moved through the organization, what tools were used at each step, and where the most significant friction lived.

Findings

What we found

The reporting process was far more fragmented and manual than leadership had understood. A typical reporting request worked like this:

  • An end user needed a report and submitted a request to a product or operations person
  • That person escalated to a technical team member, who manually pulled raw data
  • The data was formatted, usually in Tableau, and sent back upstream
  • The end user received a static output, often days later, and often had to request it again the next cycle

The core problem wasn't the tools, it was the dependency chain. Users were spending so much time waiting for reports that they had almost no time left to actually interpret what the data was telling them. They were reactive, not analytical.

A second critical finding: reporting maturity varied enormously across sub-teams. Some had already developed lightweight, functional reporting systems of their own. Others were starting from scratch. A single centralized platform forcing everyone to migrate would create massive adoption risk, especially for teams that already had something working.

Impact

How the research changed the direction

The readout reframed the product conversation entirely. Instead of rebuilding the failed standalone platform, the research supported a fundamentally different approach: build a modular data and feature layer that sub-teams could opt into from within their existing tools.

  • Teams with mature reporting systems could layer in new self-service capabilities without abandoning what worked
  • Teams earlier in their journey could adopt at their own pace
  • Self-service ad hoc reporting became possible, reducing the dependency chain that was costing everyone time
  • A shared data lake infrastructure gave every tool access to consistent, reliable data without forcing convergence on a single platform

The solution respected where teams actually were, rather than imposing a single vision of where they should be. That distinction came directly from the research.
