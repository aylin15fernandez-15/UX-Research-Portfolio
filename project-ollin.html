Consumer Research · Independent Consulting

Packaging perception testing for Ollin, an ancient grain cake mix

Ollin is a cake mix brand built around the celebration of ancient grains — a product intentionally and enthusiastically gluten-positive. But consumers kept reading the packaging as gluten-free. A structured perception study helped identify exactly what was causing the misread and gave the brand a clear path to fixing it.

Scale

50 participants

Methods

Survey research · Consumer perception testing · 1Q platform

Timeline

Summer 2024

My role

Sole researcher · Survey design · Findings delivery · Design recommendations

Background

The brand and the problem

Ollin is an independent cake mix brand founded by my sister, built around the use of ancient grains. The brand's central message is a celebration of what ancient grains bring to baking — flavor, nutrition, texture — and the packaging was designed to communicate that distinctiveness.

But as the brand approached its first year, a pattern had emerged in consumer feedback: people were encountering the product and thinking it was gluten-free. Not just occasionally — frequently enough that it was becoming a real concern. For a brand that is explicitly the opposite of gluten-free, this was a significant messaging problem.

The hypothesis was that something about the packaging design was triggering a "gluten-free" association. The question was what, exactly — and how confident we could be in that read before making design changes.

Approach

Designing the study

I designed a consumer perception survey using 1Q, a market research platform that enables targeted recruitment of specific consumer profiles. The sample was defined as adults in the United States — a broad but appropriate screen for a consumer packaged goods product available nationally.

The survey showed participants photos of the Ollin packaging and asked a series of questions designed to surface:

  • Their initial impression of the product — what kind of product it was, who it seemed designed for
  • What they believed the product's key characteristics were, including any dietary attributes they associated with it
  • Which specific elements of the packaging shaped those impressions
  • Open-ended space for anything else they noticed or interpreted

50 responses were collected — a meaningful sample for a consumer perception question of this specificity.

Findings

What the data showed

The misread was real. A meaningful portion of participants did interpret the packaging as gluten-free or associated it with gluten-free dietary positioning — confirming that the problem wasn't just anecdotal feedback.

More importantly, the research identified which specific design elements were driving the association. Not all participants misread it, and the ones who didn't pointed to different cues than the ones who did — which gave us a clear signal about what was communicating the wrong message and what wasn't.

The open-ended responses added useful texture: participants described the visual language of the packaging in ways that helped explain why certain elements triggered a gluten-free read, even when no explicit claim was being made.

Impact

From research to redesign

I delivered findings to my sister alongside specific, design-actionable recommendations — identifying which elements to reconsider and why, grounded in what participants had actually responded to.

She brought those findings to her brand designer, who combined the research insights with their own design perspective to work through the changes. The result was a packaging refresh that sharpened the visual communication of what Ollin actually is: a celebration of ancient, gluten-positive grains.
